The One AI Image Tool That Made Me Rethink My Sketchbook

I stared at a blank canvas on my tablet and muttered, “If only I could see this in color without spending hours mixing pigments.” The thought wasn’t new, but the solution that landed on my screen that night was. A quick login to Leonardo.ai turned a vague mental picture into a crisp, layered composition in under ten minutes.

The first encounter – why I was skeptical

My first brush with Leonardo was at a design meet‑up where a fellow illustrator bragged about “instant concept art.” I’d heard the hype before and dismissed it as another novelty that would crumble under a real brief. Still, curiosity nudged me to open the tool later that week, half‑expecting a glitchy mess.

Getting past the learning curve

The interface looked like a stripped‑down Photoshop panel, but the real learning happened in the prompt bar. I typed a simple description—“a rain‑soaked neon street at night, cyber‑punk vibe”—and watched as the model churned out four variations. The first batch was noisy, the colors oversaturated, but the second hit a tone that felt almost cinematic. I spent the next hour tweaking adjectives, adding “soft shadows” and “lens flare” until the output matched the mental image I’d been chasing.

The prompt language that actually works

What surprised me most was how literal the model treated spatial descriptors. When I wrote “foreground with a lone cyclist, background blurred by motion,” the cyclist appeared crisp while the cityscape melted into streaks. I learned to sandwich key subjects between commas and to use brackets for emphasis, like “[cyclist]” to force focus. The result was a reliable way to tell the AI where my eye should land.

My first real project: a book cover that needed a twist

A friend approached me to design a cover for his sci‑fi novella. He wanted a “retro‑futuristic skyline with a lone astronaut floating above.” I drafted a rough sketch, fed it into Leonardo, and asked for “high‑contrast, 1970s poster style.” Within minutes the AI produced a version with a saturated magenta sky and a stylized astronaut silhouette that felt instantly publishable. I exported the 4K PNG, opened it in Photoshop, and added the title in hand‑lettered brushwork. The final product earned a nod from the publisher’s art director, who said the “visual hook was exactly what we needed.”

Fine‑tuning with style presets and LoRA models

Leonardo offers a library of LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) models that let you graft a specific artist’s style onto a base image. I experimented with a model trained on Syd Mead’s concept art, layering it over a cityscape prompt. The building silhouettes softened, and the neon glows acquired that unmistakable Mead sheen. Adjusting the LoRA weight from 0.3 to 0.7 shifted the balance from “my original idea” to “Syd’s signature,” giving me a dial to control influence rather than an all‑or‑nothing switch.

The human feedback loop – why I still need my own eye

Even with the best prompts, the AI throws in details I never intended. In one iteration it added a flock of birds that clashed with the mood of a dystopian alley. I had to manually erase them, then feed the corrected image back into the system with a “negative prompt” that said “no birds.” This back‑and‑forth taught me that the tool is a collaborator, not a replacement; my judgment decides what stays and what goes.

A frustrating glitch that taught me patience

One afternoon I tried to generate a series of character portraits for a tabletop campaign. The model started producing identical faces with slightly different hair colors, ignoring my instruction to vary ethnicity. I traced the problem to a server overload on Leonardo’s side; the queue was backed up, and the system fell back to a cached version of my last prompt. After a half‑hour of waiting, the results finally refreshed, but the delay reminded me that no AI can outrun the latency of real‑world hardware.

Numbers that mattered – speed, cost, resolution

I tracked my usage over a month. A typical 1024×1024 render cost 0.03 credits, roughly $0.12 in real money, and took about 7 seconds to render. When I upgraded to the “Ultra” tier for 4K output, the price jumped to 0.12 credits per image, and the render time stretched to 22 seconds. Those numbers mattered when I was churning out 30 assets for a client; the total cost stayed under $15, a fraction of what a freelance illustrator would charge for the same volume.

Integrating Leonardo into my existing workflow

My usual process starts with a thumbnail sketch on paper, then a vector outline in Illustrator. With Leonardo, I now insert a quick AI‑generated texture layer before I ever open Illustrator. For a recent poster, I asked for “grunge concrete texture with subtle rust veins.” The AI delivered a seamless 4096×4096 texture that I imported directly, saving me hours of hand‑painting. I still refine the edges manually, but the bulk of the work arrives ready to blend.

The moment it fell short – a lesson in limits

I once tried to generate a realistic portrait of a senior citizen with deep wrinkles and a specific scar above the eyebrow. I fed the AI a reference photo and added “deep scar, realistic lighting.” The output captured the lighting perfectly but smoothed out the scar, rendering it as a faint line. I realized the model’s training data emphasized idealized faces, and that extreme detail could be lost. I ended up painting the scar myself in Procreate, using the AI image as a base. That honest mistake reminded me that the tool can’t replace the nuance of a skilled hand.

How it reshaped my creative process

Now I start most projects with a rapid AI brainstorming session. I throw ten wild prompts at Leonardo, pick the three that spark something, and flesh those out manually. The speed of iteration has doubled my idea generation rate; what used to take a day of sketching now happens in an hour. I still spend time polishing, but the mental load of “what could this look like?” has lifted dramatically.

What I still wish it could do

There are two gaps that keep me reaching for my own toolkit. First, the ability to lock a composition while swapping out only the color palette would save me from re‑rendering entire scenes. Second, a more granular control over lighting direction—right now I can say “soft side light,” but I can’t tell the AI to place the key light at a 45‑degree angle from the left. Until those features arrive, I’ll keep layering traditional tools on top.

Bottom line – why I keep it on my desk

Leonardo isn’t a magic wand that draws the perfect image on command, but it is a fast, flexible partner that amplifies my own vision. It forces me to articulate ideas more clearly, nudges me out of creative ruts, and hands me a high‑resolution draft that I can sculpt further. The moments when it stumbled taught me humility, and the moments when it nailed a mood saved me hours. For a creator who still values the scratch of a pencil and the decision of a single brushstroke, that balance feels just right.
